Three Good Things Exercise: How It Works, and Does It?

Short answer: the three good things exercise is simple. Every night for a week, write down three things that went well that day, and for each one, why it happened. In a 2005 study, people who did it were still happier than at the start six months later. Later studies found the effect is small. It’s a modest thing, and it costs four minutes and a pen.

A colleague leaves a biscuit on your desk without a word. The rain holds off until you’re inside. Your dad, who never phones, phones to tell you about a bird he saw.

By bedtime, if anyone asks, the day was “busy” or “all right”. The biscuit, the dry walk and the bird have slipped away. What’s still there is the bill you need to sort out in the morning.

Many of the good parts of a day are gone before you get home. This exercise is a small way of keeping a few.

What is the three good things exercise?

On an ordinary evening, it goes like this:

  1. Write down three things that went well today. Small is fine.
  2. Next to each one, write why it happened.

The train was on time, because you caught the earlier one. Your neighbour waved, because you helped with her bins last week. The bread came out well, because you left it to rise longer.

In the original study people did it every night for a week.

People often call this a gratitude journal, and it’s close. The part that’s easy to skip is the “why”. In the study, people wrote a reason for each good thing, not just the thing.

Where did it come from?

Martin Seligman, Tracy Steen, Nansook Park and Christopher Peterson reported the study in American Psychologist in 2005. They posted a link titled “Happiness Exercises” on a website, and in roughly a month 577 adults had signed up through it.

Everyone was randomly assigned one exercise to do for a week, and told it might work, or it might be a placebo that did nothing. The placebo group spent each night writing about early memories. One of the other groups was given Three Good Things.

Happiness and depressive symptoms were measured one week after the exercise week ended, then at one month, three months and six months. Of the 577 who started, 411 completed every check.

Straight after the week, every group, the placebo one included, was a bit happier. At the following check, the placebo group had slipped back to where it began. Not so the Three Good Things group. Its improvement appeared by the one-month check, and at three and six months its members were still happier and less depressed than at the outset.

The people who kept going on their own after the week were the happiest. It’s worth being careful with that one, though. They chose to keep going, so they may simply have been the people it was already helping.

Does writing down good things really work?

Honestly priced: a little, for some people, for a while.

The effect in the 2005 study was small. Those who took part had found their way to a happiness website, and they were largely white and more educated than average, so they weren’t a sample of everyone.

Myriam Mongrain and Tracy Anselmo-Matthews repeated the study in 2012, in the Journal of Clinical Psychology, adding a comparison group. People doing the exercises became happier, but so did people doing a “positive placebo”, a task designed to bring pleasant personal material to mind. For depression, the exercises did no better than that comparison. The authors’ view was that the gain comes from directing attention to good, personal things in the first place.

Linda Bolier and colleagues combined 39 trials of positive psychology exercises, covering 6,139 people, in BMC Public Health in 2013. Right after, the exercises raised wellbeing and lowered depressive symptoms. Three to six months on, the wellbeing gain remained but was smaller, and the fall in depressive symptoms was no longer significant. All the effects were small.

So I wouldn’t sell it to you as more than it is. Noting good things each night has done people a bit of good, for a while. That’s a modest result, and four minutes and a pen is a modest price.

Does telling someone help too?

You can also store a good thing in someone else.

Shelly Gable, Harry Reis, Emily Impett and Evan Asher published four studies in 2004 in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology about how people handle good news. In two, sharing a good event with others went with more positive feelings from day to day, beyond the good event on its own. The boost was bigger when the person listening reacted with genuine interest, the “go on, tell me more” kind, rather than a quick “great” and a new topic.

Notice it, write it down with why it happened, and tell one person. That’s my own combination of these studies. Nobody tested it as one routine, but each step has some research behind it.

What it can’t do

Writing down nice moments won’t mend a hard week. If your hours have just been cut, or someone close to you is unwell, a few good things jotted down at night don’t balance that out, and none of these studies claims they would. The point is to keep hold of the good things, not to shrink the bad ones.

And don’t let it turn into another chore you’re failing at. If three each night feels like too much, do one, or skip alternate nights. A habit that doesn’t feel like homework is far easier to stick with.

Tonight: one good thing and why

Do this tonight, four minutes. A notebook by the bed, or a note on your phone.

  1. Write one good thing from today. However small.
  2. Write why it happened, or who it involved.
  3. If a second and third come, write those too. If they don’t, one is enough.

Example: The bus was on time. Because I left five minutes early for once.

Tomorrow, tell one person about one of them.

Questions people ask

How long does the three good things exercise take to work? In the 2005 study people did it for one week. Everyone felt a little better straight after, including the placebo group, but the Three Good Things group’s gains showed up at the one-month check and were still there at six months.

Does it have to be three things? Three is what the study used. If three turns into a struggle, one honest line is better than giving up. Some nights there’s only one thing, and that’s fine.

Is it the same as a gratitude journal? Close. The difference in the study was writing why each good thing happened, which asks you to look at what made it go well.

Is it worth doing if the effect is small? That’s your call. The studies can’t tell you in advance whether it will help you. But it costs very little to find out.
