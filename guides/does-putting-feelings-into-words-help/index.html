Does Putting Feelings Into Words Help?

Short answer: a little, in the studies that have tested it. In a 2011 set of lab studies, people looking at unpleasant pictures reported less distress when they picked a word for the feeling than when they simply looked, even though, on average, they predicted the word would make things worse. A review of 146 writing studies found a small average benefit. Neither shows what one word will do for you on a hard day.

You’re on the top deck of the bus home, and a voice note from your brother has just finished playing. Nothing dramatic. He’s moving to Leeds in the spring, he says, and he sounds pleased. You reply with a thumbs up and a “brilliant!!” and then stare at the steamed up window for three stops with something heavy sitting under your ribs.

If someone asked what it was, you’d say “nothing, I’m just tired”. If you made yourself find the actual word, it might be “left”. Or “jealous”. Or, more simply, “sad”.

That gap between “nothing” and the real word is what this guide is about.

Does naming a feeling actually help?

The research on this goes under the name affect labelling, which just means putting a feeling into words. The best known paper in the area is “Putting Feelings Into Words”, published in Psychological Science in 2007 by Matthew Lieberman of the University of California, Los Angeles, and five colleagues. That study measured brain activity, not how people said they felt, so it isn’t the one to lean on for the question most people are asking, which is simply: will I feel any different?

For that, a later paper is more useful.

What did people say they felt?

In 2011, Matthew Lieberman, Tristen Inagaki, Golnaz Tabibnia and Molly Crockett published four studies in the journal Emotion. In the first, 44 undergraduates at the University of California, Los Angeles, looked at a series of pictures, some neutral and some very unpleasant. On some pictures they just watched. On others they chose, from two words shown underneath, the word that fitted the feeling in the picture. After each one they rated how distressed they had felt.

Distress was lower on the pictures where they picked a word than on the ones where they only watched. Two further studies compared labelling with two deliberate strategies, rethinking the picture and distracting yourself, and labelling came out with similar effects on distress.

The strangest finding was about expectations. People were asked to predict how they would feel. Unlike rethinking and distraction, labelling was not something they believed would help. Even after they had done the task and reported less distress, they still predicted, on average, that naming a feeling would make it worse next time.

A fourth study used pleasant pictures, and there labelling went with less pleasure. So a word seems to take the edge off a feeling in either direction, rather than only easing the bad ones.

What did that study not show?

Quite a lot, and it’s worth being plain about it.

The people were university students in a lab, looking at photographs for a few seconds each. They weren’t naming their own feeling about their own life. They were choosing between two words someone else had supplied, about a picture. Their distress was measured in the moment, on a rating scale, not hours or days later.

So it tells you that, on average, in that setting, picking a word lowered distress. It doesn’t tell you that naming your feeling on the bus will change your evening, or that it works for everyone, or that it works better than talking to someone.

What about writing it down at more length?

There’s a separate, much bigger body of work on writing about thoughts and feelings, often called expressive writing. In the typical study, one group writes about something personal and meaningful for a few short sessions, and another group writes about something neutral, such as how they spend their day.

In 2006, Joanne Frattaroli published a review in Psychological Bulletin that pulled together 146 randomised studies of this kind of disclosure. On average, the effect was positive and statistically significant, but small: an average effect size of r = .075. She also identified a number of factors that made the effect larger or smaller.

Small is the honest word here. Writing about a feeling helped on average across many studies, but not by much, and the review cannot say who will gain and who won’t. I read the abstract of this review, not the full paper, so I’m not going to claim anything more specific than that.

Why does it feel like it won’t help?

Partly because, in the moment, naming a feeling feels like agreeing to it. “Sad” sounds heavier than “nothing”. The 2011 studies are interesting precisely because people kept expecting the word to make things worse, and on average it didn’t.

There’s also a habit many people have of skipping straight to the reason. “I’m fine, it’s just that he’s moving and I’ll have to sort out Christmas differently.” That’s a sentence about logistics. The word underneath it never gets said.

One minute: find the word under “fine”

Try it now, one minute. You need a note on your phone or the back of a receipt.

  1. Think of one moment from today that left a bit of weight behind, however small.
  2. Write down what you’d tell someone if they asked: “fine”, “tired”, “nothing really”.
  3. Underneath, write one more exact word for what was actually there. One word, not a reason.
  4. If a second word turns up that you’d rather not write, use that one.
  5. Leave it there. You don’t have to do anything with it.

Example: Tired. Underneath: left out.

If you want a step further, write one plain sentence with that word in it to someone you trust. You can send it or keep it. Choosing a true word is the part the studies tested; the sentence is your own choice.

Questions people ask

What is affect labelling? It means putting a feeling into words, usually a single word, such as “angry” or “afraid”. In the lab studies, people often chose the word from a short list.

Is naming a feeling the same as dwelling on it? Not as it was tested. People named the feeling once and moved on to the next picture. Going over the same moment again and again is a different thing.

Does it only work for bad feelings? In the 2011 studies, naming also took some of the pleasure off people felt at nice pictures. It seems to soften a feeling in either direction.

Is writing about feelings better than just naming them? The studies don’t compare the two directly. Naming was tested in quick lab tasks; expressive writing was tested over several sessions. Both showed small effects on average.
