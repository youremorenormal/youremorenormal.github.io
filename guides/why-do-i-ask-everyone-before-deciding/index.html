Why Do I Ask Everyone Before Making a Decision?

Short answer: many people ask everyone before making a decision because asking feels like action, and while the question stays open, nothing can go wrong yet. But a choice left open for months is still a choice: to stay where you are. More opinions often pull in opposite directions. It can help more to name the facts you’re missing, then write down your own view.

You’ve been offered a place on a part time evening course that starts in January. You’ve told your mum, two friends, your manager, a cousin who did something similar, and you’ve read four forum threads.

Your mum says go for it. One friend says wait a year. Your cousin says it changed her life. Your manager says it’s entirely up to you, which you’d rather she hadn’t. The forums say everything.

The deadline is Friday. You now have seven opinions, and you feel less sure than you did before you asked the first person.

Why do I always ask other people before making decisions?

There are a few ordinary reasons, and none of them means you’re weak.

  • Asking feels like progress. Sending one more message feels like you’re working on it, even when nothing moves.
  • It shares the weight. If someone else tells you what to do and it goes badly, it isn’t all on you.
  • It keeps the door open. As long as you’re still collecting views, nothing is decided, and a choice that hasn’t been made can’t be the wrong one.

That last one feels safe. The catch is that time keeps passing while you ask. A decision you hold open for a year is still made, just quietly, one evening at a time, and nobody ever says it out loud.

Does asking more people help?

Sometimes, at first. After that, extra opinions tend to cancel each other out. One person says go and another says wait. One says trust your gut, the next says never trust your gut. In my view the fifth opinion rarely adds much to the first, and it can give your worry more to work with.

And the view that gets lost in all that noise is your own. If the loop of asking is where you are right now, the page on how to decide when you keep asking everyone is written for exactly that.

When is asking the right move?

Not every decision is yours to make alone, and not every question is about what you want. Some decisions are waiting on a fact you don’t have yet: what a doctor says, what a lawyer says, what’s in a bank statement, or how the person involved really feels, which needs a proper conversation. Some need an expert, and booking one is a clear decision in its own right.

A plain way to tell the difference:

  • A question someone else can answer better than you. What does it cost? What are the rules? Is there another intake in the spring? Ask it. That’s information.
  • A question about what you want. Should I do it? Will I regret it? Nobody else lives your life, so other people can only guess. That’s the kind of question that keeps getting passed around.

Even when you need a fact, a steadier part of you can usually name the next step: make the phone call, book the appointment, or give it a week.

How do I trust my own decisions?

Start by noticing how you talk when a friend asks you for advice. You don’t list every risk, and you don’t say “it depends” five times. You pick out the one or two things that matter, say them plainly, and add something kind because you can see they’re anxious. That voice belongs to you. It doesn’t vanish when the problem is your own; it just gets harder to hear.

Researchers have found the same gap. In 2014, Igor Grossmann and Ethan Kross called it “Solomon’s paradox”, in Psychological Science: in their lab studies, people reasoned more wisely about a friend’s trouble in a close relationship than about their own. They used set scenarios, so it’s one piece of evidence rather than a rule for every choice. The guide on why it’s easier to give advice than take it explains that study, and the small change that closed the gap in the lab.

Trusting your decision doesn’t mean feeling certain. A clear answer is often short, and it can feel calm rather than exciting. It might be “not this year”, or “send the email”, or “ask about deferring”.

What if the answer is one I don’t want?

Sometimes the reason we can’t hear our own answer is that we don’t like it. Give up the course. Tell your sister no. Stop paying for the gym you don’t go to. Often the answer was never hidden. It just wasn’t the one we were hoping for, so we keep asking in case somebody says something different.

If you notice you’re asking the same question of a sixth person, it’s worth asking yourself what you’d like them to say, and why.

Ten minutes: a letter from your clearest self

Try this when you have ten quiet minutes. A sheet of paper is best, but a note on your phone works.

  1. At the top, write “Dear” and your own name. Write as the calmer version of you, the one who’d know what to say if this were a friend’s life. Use “you”, not “I”.
  2. Say what you can see from there: what matters, what doesn’t, and a single step to take in the next seven days. Half a page at most.
  3. Read it back once. Find the sentence you’d rather it hadn’t said, and copy that one out on its own.

Example: Dear Sam, you’ve asked seven people and the only voice you keep arguing with is your own. You want the course. What scares you is the money, not the work. This week, email the college and ask if you can pay in instalments, then decide by Friday.

That uncomfortable sentence is the one to sit with before you message anyone else. This exercise comes from the book, not from a study. What it gives you is your own view, written down, before the next opinion arrives.

Questions people ask

Is it bad to ask for advice before a big decision? No. Asking for facts you don’t have is sensible, and so is asking an expert. The trouble starts when asking takes the place of deciding, and the question goes round and round with no step at the end.

How many opinions should I get? There’s no magic number, and this page won’t invent one. A useful check: if a new opinion only adds to the noise and doesn’t give you a fact you lacked, you’ve probably heard enough.

Why do I feel more confused after asking more people? Because their views often point different ways, and each one is a guess about a life they don’t live. Your own view gets buried under theirs.

What if I make the wrong choice? That fear is real, and some choices do turn out badly. But not choosing is a choice as well, usually the one to stay put. Writing down your own view first means that whatever you decide, you decided it.
