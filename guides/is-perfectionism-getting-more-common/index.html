Is Perfectionism Getting More Common?

Short answer: among university students in the US, Canada and the UK, yes. A 2019 review combined 164 groups of students, 41,641 people in all, who filled in the same questionnaire between 1989 and 2016. Average scores rose on all three types it measures. It’s late on a Wednesday and you’re scrolling past a stranger’s tidy desk, a stranger’s finished marathon, a stranger’s “just a little update: promoted again”. You close the app and look at your own list. Half of it has been there since March. The thought arrives without being invited: everyone else seems to be meeting a standard nobody told me about.

Is that feeling something new, or has it always been around? One research team tried to find out by counting.

Is perfectionism getting more common?

Thomas Curran and Andrew Hill published a study in 2019 in Psychological Bulletin. They didn’t run a new experiment. They gathered earlier studies that had all used the same questionnaire, the Multidimensional Perfectionism Scale, and lined up the scores by the year the students were born and tested.

The pool was 164 samples and 41,641 college students from the United States, Canada and the United Kingdom, who completed the questionnaire between 1989 and 2016. According to the abstract, about 71 per cent were female and the average age was around 21.

Their finding: average scores rose in a straight line over those years on all three types the scale measures. They kept rising when the researchers allowed for gender and for differences between the three countries.

What are the three types?

The questionnaire separates perfectionism by where the demand comes from.

  • Self-oriented: you expect a lot of yourself.
  • Other-oriented: you expect a lot of other people.
  • Socially prescribed: you feel that other people expect a lot of you.

Ranked by size of increase, the abstract puts it this way. The authors say young people today perceive that others are more demanding of them, are more demanding of others, and are more demanding of themselves. So the biggest rise was in the feeling that other people expect a great deal of you, and the smallest was in expecting a lot of yourself.

What this does not show

Four limits are worth keeping in view.

It’s students. The data come from university students. They say nothing directly about people who never went to university, older adults, or other countries.

It’s self-rated. Everyone filled in a questionnaire about themselves. A higher score means people described themselves as more perfectionistic, which isn’t the same as being measured doing perfect work.

Different people each time. This isn’t one group followed for 27 years. Each wave is a fresh set of students, so the finding is about each new cohort compared with the last, not about anyone getting more perfectionistic as they aged.

It describes a trend, not a cause. The abstract opens with an idea about competitive culture in these three countries, and the authors tested whether scores rose. That the scores rose is the finding. Why they rose is a separate question that this kind of analysis can’t settle.

Does a higher score mean worse outcomes?

Not from this study alone. It tells you where scores went, not what the scores do to people. A separate review is the better place to look. Andrew Hill and Thomas Curran combined 43 studies in 2016, with 9,838 people, looking at perfectionism and burnout in work, sport and education. Aiming high had a small negative or non-significant link with burnout. Worry about mistakes and judgement had a medium to large positive link. The authors flagged that future work should use longitudinal designs, so those links are still associations rather than proof of cause.

The guide on healthy striving versus perfectionism goes through that second study in detail.

Two minutes: whose standard is this?

Try it now, two minutes. You need a pen and a scrap of paper, or a note on your phone.

  1. Write down one standard you’re holding yourself to this week. A tidy inbox, a polished presentation, a spotless flat.
  2. Next to it, write whose it is. Pick one: mine, someone I expect it of, or someone I think expects it of me.
  3. If the answer is the third, write the person or group. “My manager” or “people online” both count.
  4. Ask one question: would I judge a friend by this standard? Write yes or no.

Example: “Reply to every message within the hour. Whose? People who I think expect it of me, my team. Would I judge a friend by it? No, I’d think they were busy.”

You don’t have to drop the standard. The point is only to see whose it is before you decide how much of your week it deserves.

Questions people ask

Is perfectionism increasing in young people? In this review of university students in the US, Canada and the UK, average scores rose from 1989 to 2016 on all three types measured. The study didn’t cover young people who weren’t in college.

What kind of perfectionism has risen the most? Socially prescribed perfectionism, the feeling that others expect a lot of you, showed the largest increase according to the abstract.

Why is perfectionism increasing? The study didn’t test a cause. The authors raise cultural change as an idea, but a pooled comparison of scores over time can’t show that this is what happened.

Does this mean I’m a perfectionist? No. The figures are averages across tens of thousands of students and say nothing about any one person.
