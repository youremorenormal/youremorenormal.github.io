How to Text a Friend You Haven’t Spoken To in Years

Short answer: to text a friend you haven’t spoken to in years, keep it to a couple of lines, mention one particular thing you remember or value about them, and ask for nothing back. “Walked past our old flat today and thought of you. Hope you’re well.” is enough. Research on reaching out suggests people are often more pleased to hear from you than you expect.

There’s someone you think about now and then. An old flatmate, a cousin you were close to as kids, the person who sat next to you at your first job. You’ve had their number for years. Every so often you open the chat, look at the date of the last message, and close it again.

It’s been too long, you tell yourself. It would be strange now. They’ve probably moved on. They’ll wonder what you want.

So the gap gets another year longer.

Will they think I’m annoying?

This is often the worry that stops the message, and it’s one there’s research on.

Peggy Liu, SoYon Rim, Lauren Min and Kate Min published “The Surprise of Reaching Out: Appreciated More Than We Think” in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology in 2023. In one of their experiments, students on a US campus picked a fellow student they liked but had drifted out of contact with, and wrote them a brief note just to say hello and see how they were. Each writer then guessed, from 1 to 7, how much the note would be appreciated. Later the recipient, who was told the note came from a classmate taking part in a study, read it and rated their own appreciation on that same 1 to 7 scale.

By the end, 54 writer and recipient pairs had given both answers. On average the writers predicted roughly 5.6. The recipients’ average was roughly 6.2. The difference is modest, but the writers as a whole aimed too low.

Across the paper there are thirteen preregistered studies, which means the researchers published their plan before collecting any data, and the main ones showed the same pattern. The biggest involved 1,602 adults recruited online in the US and the UK, who pictured either giving a small gift to someone real in their life or getting one from them. People underestimated the response for close friends and for more distant contacts alike, and missed by most for the more distant ones.

The authors point to surprise as one reason. Recipients paid more attention to how surprised they were than senders did. Someone you haven’t spoken to in years is exactly the person who won’t see your message coming, and that’s no bad thing.

You think it will be awkward

Amit Kumar and Nicholas Epley found a similar thing with thank-you letters, in “Undervaluing Gratitude”, a 2018 paper in Psychological Science. In three experiments, the people writing predicted their readers would feel less happy and more uncomfortable than the readers actually said they did. And the more awkward people expected it to be, the less keen they were to write at all.

I should be honest about the limits. Nobody tested your exact message, and nobody measured “annoying”. The campus study was about friends who’d been out of touch for a while, not for years, and the friends knew it was part of a study. But the worry that holds you back, they’ll find it weird, is the same kind of guess these studies found to be too gloomy. It’s worth holding it a bit more loosely. If you’d like more on why reaching out feels so hard, the page on reaching out when it feels awkward goes further.

How to text a friend you haven’t spoken to in years: three first lines

Keep the recipe short. Pick one particular thing, a memory you share or something about them you value. Don’t ask them for anything, not even a coffee. Send it and leave it there.

You don’t need to explain the gap or apologise for it. The specific thing does the work, because it tells them they stayed in your mind.

Change these until they sound like you.

A memory:

“Heard that song from the Leeds trip on the radio this morning and laughed out loud in the car. Hope you’re well.”

A thank you you never said:

“Still use the trick you showed me for fixing a stuck zip. Thought of you today when it saved my coat. Thank you, years late.”

Simply that they came to mind:

“You came into my head today and I realised it’s been far too long. No need to reply, I just wanted to say hello.”

Two or three lines is plenty. No question they have to answer, so a reply is a pleasure for them, not a job.

Why knowing this isn’t enough

There’s a finding in the Liu paper that’s easy to miss. In a supplemental study, 118 students were told what the research had shown, that people value being contacted more than the person getting in touch predicts. Hearing that didn’t raise their interest in reaching out. Nor did it make them any more likely to send a note they had just drafted, within a short time limit during the study.

One possibility the authors raise is that people mean to get in touch, put it off, and then it slips their mind. The text you’re going to send at the weekend has a way of becoming, by Monday, the text you were going to send. So the exercise below is about pressing send, not making a plan.

One minute: send it now

Try this now, one minute or less. Get your phone out.

  1. Bring to mind someone you like and have lost touch with. Go with whoever turns up first.
  2. Type a couple of lines that mention one particular memory or one thing you’re grateful for. Leave out any request.
  3. Send it. One read through, no more.

If you get a reply, good. If you don’t, you still sent it.

Questions people ask

Is it weird to message someone after years? It may feel it. But in the studies above, people who reached out tended to underestimate how much it would be appreciated, and in the largest one the guess was furthest off for more distant people rather than close friends.

What if they don’t reply? It stings, but no reply isn’t a verdict on you. People are busy, and some replies never get sent. A message that asks for nothing back means silence costs you very little.

Should I suggest meeting up in the first message? I’d leave it out. A first message that asks for nothing is easier to send and easier to receive. If they reply warmly, there’s time to suggest a coffee then.

What if I want to tell them I’m going through a hard time? That’s a different message, and a brave one. There’s a separate guide on what to say to a friend when you’re struggling.
