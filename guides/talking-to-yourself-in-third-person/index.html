Does Talking to Yourself in the Third Person Help?

Short answer: a little, and in a useful way. Talking to yourself in the third person, using “you” or your own name instead of “I”, helps you step back from a stressful moment. In a set of seven studies, people who did it before a stressful task felt less distress and were judged to do better. It isn’t a cure. It’s one small shift you can make in your head.

It sounds like something you’d keep quiet about. Sitting on the edge of the bed before a hard conversation and thinking, Right, Sam. Why are you so nervous about this? It feels a bit daft to describe.

But everyone chats away to themselves. Bins go out tonight. Second door on the right. Most of that is harmless and handy. The kind that hurts is the running commentary after a mistake or a comparison, which sounds so like your own thoughts that you take it as fact.

Why did you say that?

Everyone else managed it.

You should be further on by now.

This page is about one small way of answering that voice, and what the research does and doesn’t say about it.

Why talking to yourself in the third person can help

Your own problem has no outside. You’re in the middle of it, with the money, the fear of what people think and the old plans you haven’t met all crowding in together, and with everything that close, it’s hard to make out what matters. A friend’s problem is something you hear about once. Your own keeps playing.

Hand the same problem to someone else and you see it from across the room. The important part is obvious, and you can find a gentle way to say it.

Switching from “I” to “you”, or to your own name, borrows a bit of that outside view. You’re not pretending to be someone else. You’re just giving yourself a few feet of distance, much like the distance you’d have if a friend had brought you the problem.

What did the studies actually find?

In 2014, Ethan Kross of the University of Michigan and colleagues published seven studies in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology.

The tasks were designed to put people under pressure: giving a speech, or making a first impression on someone they’d never met. Beforehand, some people thought about it using “I” (Why am I nervous?). Others used “you” or their first name (Why is Sam nervous?).

Using “you” or a name helped people step back from the situation. Observers rated their performance as better. They reported less distress, went over it less afterwards, and looked at what was coming as a challenge rather than a threat.

I should be honest about the limits. These were tasks set by researchers, not your own Monday morning, so treat them as one piece of evidence rather than the last word. Changing one word won’t fix a hard week. What the studies suggest is that it can take some of the heat out of a stressful moment.

The same gap shows up when you give advice

There’s a related finding I find just as useful. Also in 2014, Igor Grossmann of the University of Waterloo and the same Ethan Kross gave a name to something many people will recognise: being wiser about other people’s problems than about your own. They called it “Solomon’s Paradox”, in the journal Psychological Science.

People in long-term relationships imagined their partner cheating, or a friend’s partner cheating. When it was the friend’s situation, they reasoned more wisely: they owned up to what they couldn’t know, pictured several possible outcomes, and searched for a compromise. When it was their own partner, they did that less.

Next, some people were asked to consider their own situation in the third person, wondering why he or she felt this way rather than why I did. Their reasoning about their own problem then matched other people’s reasoning about a friend’s. A third study found this in both younger and older adults. All of it was done in a lab with set relationship scenarios, so it’s no promise that one trick fixes every decision.

If you’ve ever sorted out a friend’s problem in four minutes and circled your own for a year, there’s more on that in how to decide when you keep asking everyone.

Is this the same as positive self-talk?

Not quite, and the difference matters.

The critical voice is sometimes right. The report was late. The car does need a service. Reply with “I’m brilliant” or “who cares” and you won’t believe yourself, so the voice comes out on top. Kindness that isn’t true doesn’t help. Fairness does.

Being fair means you keep the fact and throw out the judgement attached to it. “The report was late” is a fact. “I’m useless at this job” is the voice’s addition.

Using your name helps here because it’s how you’d talk to a friend. You wouldn’t tell a friend they’re brilliant at everything. You’d say, “You were late with one report. What held it up?” Honest and kind together, which is the hardest tone to find when the person is you.

If the voice in your head is harsh most days, the page on why you’re so hard on yourself goes further into where it comes from.

Two minutes: answer the voice by name

Try this now, two minutes. Paper, or a fresh note on your phone.

  1. Copy down the unkindest thing the voice said to you today, word for word.
  2. On the line below, put your first name followed by a comma.
  3. Carry on as a good friend would reply. Hold on to whatever is true. Leave out the judgement.

Example: “You always mess these things up.” Becomes: “Priya, you got one thing wrong this week. That’s true. It isn’t the same as always.”

Read the new line once. It will likely sound steadier than the old one. That’s what a little distance does.

Questions people ask

Is it weird to talk to yourself in the third person? It can feel odd at first, but nobody else needs to know. In the studies, people simply thought it through using “you” or their name before a stressful task. It can stay inside your head.

Should I use “you” or my name? Either. In the Kross studies, people who used “you” and people who used their own first name both stepped back from the situation. Pick whichever feels less awkward.

Does it stop the inner critic? No. The voice won’t go silent. You can, though, learn to notice it sooner and reply to it better. Answering it by name is one fair way to reply.

Is there brain research behind this? The studies here didn’t look at the brain, and this page doesn’t make claims about it. The findings here are about how people felt, how they saw the task ahead, and how outside raters judged them.
