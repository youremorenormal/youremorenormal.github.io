Why Is It Easier to Give Advice Than Take It?

Short answer: it’s easier to give advice than take it because you see a friend’s problem from the outside, where the important parts stand out, and you live inside your own, where everything is loud at once. In a 2014 lab study, people who thought about their own problem from the outside, as if it belonged to someone else, reasoned about it as wisely as people did about a friend’s.

A friend rings you about the course she’s been doing for two years and no longer wants. She’s worried about the money she’s already spent, and about what her mum will say. Before she’s even finished, you know what you’d tell her. Talk to the course office this week. The money is spent either way. Her mum will cope. You say it kindly, because you can hear she’s scared, and she says, “You’re right. I know you’re right.”

Then you put the phone down and think about your own job, the one you’ve been unhappy in for a year. And you have no idea what to do.

If that sounds familiar, there’s nothing wrong with your judgement. You’re just standing very close to your own life.

So why is it easier to give advice than take it?

A friend’s problem reaches you as one story, told once. From out there you can spot the one or two parts that count, you can tell that her fear is bigger than the facts, and the next small step is plain to see.

Your own problem doesn’t arrive as a story. You wake up in it. The bills, the guilt, what your family might say, the younger you who expected more by now: every piece of it sits right up close and talks over the others, and the noise hides the shape. Your friend’s trouble gets one telling. Yours runs all day in the background.

That’s also why asking more people rarely settles it. Asking feels like doing something. It also keeps the decision open, and as long as you haven’t chosen, you can’t choose wrong. But one person says go, another says wait, and the fifth opinion rarely helps more than the first. Your own view, the one you’d hand a friend in four minutes, ends up buried under everyone else’s. If that’s the loop you’re in, the page on deciding when you keep asking everyone goes into it.

What is Solomon’s paradox?

Researchers have a name for being sensible about other people’s troubles and lost in your own. Igor Grossmann of the University of Waterloo and Ethan Kross of the University of Michigan gave it the name “Solomon’s Paradox” in a 2014 paper in Psychological Science. King Solomon was known for settling other people’s quarrels wisely, and much less known for running his own life well, which is where the name comes from.

In their study, volunteers in long-term relationships pictured a partner cheating. For some it was their own partner, for others a friend’s. Thinking about the friend’s situation, people reasoned more wisely: they were readier to say what they couldn’t know, they could see it turning out more than one way, and they looked for a compromise. Thinking about their own partner, they did less of all three.

Does talking about yourself in the third person help?

This is the part worth borrowing. In the next step, the researchers asked some people to look at their own betrayal as an outsider would, in the third person. Instead of asking themselves why I feel this way, they asked why he or she feels this way.

With that one change, people’s reasoning about their own problem was as wise as other people’s reasoning about a friend’s. The gap was gone. A third study found the same in both younger and older adults.

I should be honest about the limits. Everything here happened in a lab, with set relationship scenarios, and none of it promises that one trick fixes every decision. Others have looked since. In 2022, Xu, Zhang and Wang ran a study online and found people judged wise ways of thinking a little more useful when the conflict was a friend’s than when it was their own. And in 2023 Murdoch and colleagues, in Stress and Health, combined 25 experiments on taking a step back from a stressful event that was already over. On average there was a small advantage, though the authors said that, given the quality of the evidence, the benefit is uncertain.

So it isn’t magic. It’s a small shift in where you stand, and it costs three minutes.

How to take your own advice

A few things help before you try it.

  • You won’t always have the answer. Some decisions are waiting on a fact: a figure from the bank, a doctor’s view, an honest talk with the person concerned. Even so, the steadier part of you can usually name the next move, like making the call or giving it seven days.
  • The answer may not be the one you wanted. Often we can’t hear it because we’d rather not. So we go on asking, hoping someone will give us a different one.
  • It may be smaller than you expect. Not “change your life”, more like “sleep on it”. Clear answers tend to be short, and they often feel quiet rather than thrilling.

Three minutes: answer it as a friend

Try it now, three minutes. If you’re worn out or too upset to think clearly, save it for tomorrow. The steadier part of you isn’t going anywhere.

  1. Grab a scrap of paper, or start a new note on your phone.
  2. Set out your problem in a few sentences, written as though a friend were telling it to you. Write in their words: “My sister wants me to move back home to help, and I keep saying yes, but…”
  3. Below it, start a reply: “If a friend told me this, I’d say…” Give yourself three minutes on a timer and keep the pen moving. No polishing. Put down exactly what you’d send them.

When the three minutes are up, read what you wrote, once. Chances are it sounds more certain than you feel. That certainty is the distance at work: for a few minutes you stood roughly where you’d stand if the problem were a friend’s.

Then take the one step your reply suggested, or put it in your diary for this week.

Questions people ask

Is Solomon’s paradox real? It’s the name Grossmann and Kross gave in 2014 to a gap they found in lab studies: people reasoned more wisely about a friend’s relationship problem than their own. A later online study found people saw wise ways of thinking as a bit more useful for a friend’s conflict than for one of their own. It’s a pattern seen in studies, not a law.

Why do I keep asking everyone for advice? Asking feels like progress, and while you’re still asking, nothing has been chosen, so nothing can go wrong yet. But leaving a choice open for a year quietly chooses something too: staying put.

Do I have to talk to myself out loud in the third person? No. In the study it was a way of thinking about the problem, asking why “he or she” felt this way. Writing your problem as if a friend had sent it is the book’s way of borrowing that outside view on paper.
