Self-Compassion and Motivation: Is Being Kind to Yourself Just Letting Yourself Off the Hook?

Short answer: not in the research the book draws on. When Juliana Breines and Serena Chen tested self-compassion and motivation in four experiments in 2012, kindness about a failure or a mistake didn’t make people give up. In one, students given a kind sentence after a hard test studied longer than students given nothing extra.

Something goes wrong. A driving test you failed on a roundabout, a funding bid that came back with a polite no, a friendship that went quiet after an argument you started. And close behind the disappointment comes a second worry.

If I go easy on myself about this, won’t I just stop trying?

Some people hang on to the harsh voice deliberately, believing it’s the thing that keeps them going. You should have known. Typical. Do better. It feels like discipline. Being kind to yourself feels like the soft option, the thing people do when they’ve given up.

This page is about whether that’s true.

Why does being kind to yourself feel like cheating?

Partly because we mix up two different things: being kind about a setback, and pretending it didn’t happen.

Sometimes you did play a part. You didn’t practise the hill start, or you sent the bid without a second pair of eyes. Rarely the whole story, but a piece of it. Owning that piece is different from beating yourself up over it, and it’s the one piece you can do something about next time.

The harsh voice blurs the two. It warns you that if you ease off on yourself, you’ll stop noticing your part, and stop trying. Whether kindness makes people stop trying is what the research looked at.

What did the study actually find?

Juliana Breines and Serena Chen, at the University of California, Berkeley, put this to the test in 2012, in four experiments reported in Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin.

In one of them, students sat a difficult vocabulary test, and most did poorly. Afterwards they were divided into three groups.

  1. The first group got a brief, kind note pointing out that lots of students struggle with this test.
  2. The second got a note designed to boost their self-esteem, saying they had to be clever to have got into this university.
  3. The third got no note at all.

Next, each student was handed words with their definitions, warned that a second test was coming, and left to study for however long they chose.

Those who’d had the kind note put in more study time than those who’d had no note. Kindness about failing didn’t make them quit. It sent them back to the work for longer.

Other experiments in the paper found that people encouraged to be kind to themselves about something they’d done wrong in the past were keener to put it right and to avoid doing it again.

That last part is worth pausing on. The fear is that kindness lets you off the hook. In these experiments, it went the other way: kindness came with more motivation to put things right, not less.

Self-compassion and motivation on the bad days

A second study looked at ordinary life rather than a test in a lab.

In 2014, Hope, Koestner and Milyavskaya reported on first-year university students in Canada, in Self and Identity. Self-compassion here means treating yourself kindly when things go wrong.

On days when their progress towards their goals slipped, students high in self-compassion tended not to feel worse. Students low in self-compassion did.

That study watched what happened rather than changing anything, so it can’t show that the kindness caused the steadier days. But it sits comfortably alongside the Breines and Chen results.

If a setback is still fresh for you, the page on coping with rejection and setbacks covers the first evening and the morning after.

Kind doesn’t mean pretending

None of this asks you to tell yourself it doesn’t matter. You cared, so of course it hurts this much, and nobody has the right to argue you out of that.

And some of what the harsh voice says is true. You did fail the test. You did skip the practice. If you answer that with “I’m amazing”, part of you knows it isn’t honest, and the voice wins the argument.

The book’s rule is simple: keep the true part, drop the verdict. “I failed this test” is a fact. “I’ll never pass anything” is a sentence the voice added.

Look again at what the kind message in the experiment actually said. It didn’t say “you did brilliantly”. It said the test is one lots of students find tough. That’s true and it’s gentle, and the students who read it studied longer than those who read nothing.

Two minutes: write it the way someone on your side would

Try this now, two minutes. Pick the most recent thing that didn’t go your way, large or small. On paper or in a phone note, write four brief lines.

  1. What happened. Just the facts, one sentence.
  2. What I did next. Even if all you did was get to bedtime.
  3. The part that was mine, if any. A single line. Skip it if there wasn’t one.
  4. What I’d do again. One thing that went right.

Example: Failed my driving test on the roundabout. Booked the next slot that evening. I hadn’t practised that junction. I’d keep how calm I stayed on the motorway bit.

Read it through. It ought to sound less like a prosecution and more like a brief note from someone who’s on your side. That’s the tone the students in the experiment were offered, in a single kind sentence.

Questions people ask

Is self-compassion the same as self-esteem? No. Self-esteem is about how good you think you are. Self-compassion, as the book uses it, is treating yourself kindly when things go wrong, whatever you think of your abilities. In the vocabulary experiment they were tested as two separate messages.

What if the setback really was my fault? Then say so, plainly, once. Owning your part is part of being fair to yourself. In the Breines and Chen experiments, people who were kind to themselves about a past wrong were keener, not less keen, to put it right and not do it again.

Won’t I lose my edge if I stop being hard on myself? That’s the exact worry the research tested, and in these experiments kindness didn’t make people give up. They’re experiments with set tasks, not your whole working life, so treat them as evidence rather than a promise. But they’re a good reason to try the kinder version for a week and see.

How do I stop being so hard on myself after a mistake? You probably won’t switch the harsh voice off. What you can do is answer it: keep the true part, drop the verdict, and write the mistake down the way you’d write it for a friend. There’s more on that voice in why am I so hard on myself.
