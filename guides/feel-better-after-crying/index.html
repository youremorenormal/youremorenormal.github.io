Why Do I Feel Better After Crying, Only Sometimes?

Short answer: why do I feel better after crying one day and not the next? Because a cry isn’t a reliable fix. In a 2011 diary study, 97 women recorded 1,004 crying episodes, and mood improved afterwards in about a third of them. In the rest it didn’t. Mood lifted more often when one other person was there.

Maybe you’ve had both. A phone call, then ten minutes in the car with the engine off, and afterwards you felt lighter, as if something had been put down. And another time, alone in the bathroom with the tap running so nobody would hear, and afterwards you felt just as heavy, only now with a blotchy face.

If the second kind has happened to you, you’re not crying wrong. In the one diary study my book draws on, it was the more common outcome.

Why do I feel better after crying some days and not others?

Sometimes a cry helps. It helps less often than you may have heard.

Lauren Bylsma, Marcel Croon, Ad Vingerhoets and Jonathan Rottenberg, of the University of South Florida and Tilburg University in the Netherlands, had 97 women keep a diary of every time they cried. The results appeared in the Journal of Research in Personality in 2011.

Across 1,004 recorded cries, mood was better afterwards roughly one time in three. The other times, it wasn’t.

It’s one study, and everyone in it was a woman, so it can’t speak for everybody. But it’s a real count of real cries, and it’s a long way from the idea that a good cry always clears the air.

You’ll find confident explanations online about what tears do inside the body. My book doesn’t make those claims, and I’m not going to make them here.

Does it matter who’s there?

The diaries showed a link between how people felt after crying and who was with them at the time.

That’s a link, not proof. People choose when to cry in company, and the days you can cry in front of someone may already be different days. Still, it suggests crying alone and crying with someone else there may not be the same thing.

So if every cry you have happens behind a closed door, it may be worth asking whether what’s missing is a person rather than the tears.

It needn’t be a big moment. It can be telling a colleague in the lift, “Sorry, bit of a rough week, my dad’s not well,” and letting your voice crack. Decide ahead of time who you’d turn to, your brother, maybe, or the friend who always picks up. If you’ve already chosen, it’s easier to reach for them when the day comes.

Why do I feel worse after crying sometimes?

The diary study can’t tell you why any one cry didn’t help. What it does tell you is that a cry that leaves you no better is ordinary. Most of the episodes in the study weren’t followed by better mood.

So if you’ve cried and felt flat afterwards, it isn’t a sign you did it wrong, or that crying is pointless for you. It’s one cry. Some sadness doesn’t lift with a good cry.

Why does a small thing make me cry?

Sadness, and grief especially, has a way of showing up long after the day itself, in the middle of something ordinary. A jingle on the radio while you’re queuing at the post office. An old coat in the hall cupboard with a bus ticket still in the pocket.

Lots of grown ups have a practised routine for holding tears back. Blink hard, swallow, start counting something. It does the job, if the job is not being seen. For a lot of people the feeling hasn’t gone anywhere, and the next small trigger brings it straight back.

Sometimes holding it in is the right call. You might be in a meeting, or with someone who wouldn’t be gentle, and picking your moment is fine. The problem is when the moment never comes.

Is there another way to let a feeling out?

There’s a second way, which is to name it.

My book mentions a 2007 study by Matthew Lieberman and colleagues, “Putting Feelings Into Words”, in Psychological Science. It was a brain-scanning study, and the people in it named the emotion shown in a picture, not their own, and it didn’t measure how they felt afterwards. So I won’t lean on it here. I still think naming the feeling is worth a try. A single word will do: lonely, or scared. Honest is better than clever.

Two minutes: name one feeling in one word

Try this now, two minutes. Paper, or a note on your phone.

  1. Pause what you’re doing. If you can, set your phone aside.
  2. Ask, quietly, “What’s the feeling right now?”
  3. Write a single word for it. No sentence, no explanation.
  4. If more than one word turns up, choose the one you’re more reluctant to write.
  5. Sit with it for a few seconds. Don’t try to sort it out.

Examples: tired. Jealous. Lost.

If the word you wrote wasn’t the one you expected, notice that.

Questions people ask

Is crying good for you? It isn’t something to fix or be ashamed of. But the research in my book doesn’t support the idea that a cry always makes you feel better. In one diary study, mood improved after about a third of cries.

Is it better to cry alone or with someone? In that study, how people felt afterwards was linked to who was with them. It can’t show that company causes the lift, but it’s a reason not to always wait until you’re alone.

Is it normal to cry over small things? Sometimes the small thing is carrying something bigger, like grief or a long week. Some people cry easily and some hardly at all, and neither is the right amount.

Why don’t I feel better after a good cry? Because most cries in the diary study weren’t followed by better mood. It isn’t a sign that something is wrong with how you cry.
