How Common Is Imposter Syndrome, Really?

Short answer: how common is imposter syndrome? Very common, but there is no single honest number. A 2020 review of 62 studies, with 14,161 people between them, found the share reporting impostor feelings ran from 9 to 82 per cent, depending largely on the questionnaire and cut-off each study used. In some studies, most people reported it.

So if you’ve been wondering about this because you’re sure you’re the only one faking it at work, you’re in a lot of company. How much company depends on who’s counting and how.

It’s your third week at the new office. In a meeting, someone asks you a question about a spreadsheet you set up, and you answer, and they nod and move on. All afternoon you go back over that nod. Was it a polite nod? Everyone else here seems to know exactly what they’re doing. You think you only got the job because you happened to interview well that day. One of these weeks, somebody’s going to realise.

Nobody said any of that to you. You said it all to yourself, between the meeting and home time.

What is imposter syndrome?

It’s the everyday name for the fear of being exposed. It’s the belief that what you’ve achieved came down to luck, or to fooling people, and that eventually someone will spot it.

You’ll see it spelled two ways, imposter and impostor. My book uses “impostor”. They mean the same thing.

It isn’t a medical diagnosis. The idea goes back to a 1978 paper by Clance and Imes, which looked at high-achieving women. Researchers have since studied it in many more groups of people.

How common is imposter syndrome, according to the research?

Dena Bravata and colleagues brought together 62 studies covering 14,161 people, in a 2020 review in the Journal of General Internal Medicine. They found impostor feelings in both men and women, at a range of ages, and among people in work.

Depending on the study, between 9 and 82 per cent of people reported the feeling.

That’s an enormous spread, and it isn’t a sign that some groups are coping while others are in pieces. Mostly, it reflects how each study went about measuring it.

Why do imposter syndrome statistics vary so much?

The answer you get depends a lot on the question you ask. The studies didn’t all use the same questionnaire, and they set different thresholds for deciding who counted as having the feeling. Set the bar low and plenty of people clear it. Set it high and many fewer do.

So when you see one neat percentage quoted on its own, with no questionnaire and no cut-off behind it, it’s worth holding loosely. The honest answer is a range, and a wide one.

Why the range is reassuring

Here’s the part I find useful. Even the careful version of the answer is “a lot of people”. In some studies most people reported it. It showed up in men and in women, at different ages, in the workplace.

That changes what the thought means. When it arrives, you can take it as an ordinary human response, not an assessment of how good you are. If you feel it, you’re far from the only one.

Does knowing it’s common actually help?

There’s research on a related idea: the worry about whether you belong somewhere new.

Gregory Walton and Geoffrey Cohen of Stanford University published a study in Science in 2011. It involved 92 first-year students at a university in the United States, Black and white, randomly assigned to groups. Some of them read accounts from older students at their university about starting out: in the early days, everybody had doubted whether they fitted in, and over time, everybody settled. They then wrote about how this matched their own experience, addressed to future new students. It was a single session.

Over the next three years the researchers tracked the students’ grades. For the Black students, whom the researchers had expected to benefit most, grades went up relative to several control groups, and the difference between Black and white students’ grades shrank by roughly half. The researchers’ explanation was that the message changed the way students interpreted hard days.

Later work involved thousands of students. A 2023 trial, led by Walton with a large team and also published in Science, included 26,911 students across 22 institutions. The session increased the proportion of students who completed their first year full time, but only where the students’ groups had genuine opportunities to belong. Elsewhere the effect was smaller, or absent.

These were university students, not a new job or a first shift on a ward. What follows borrows their idea rather than a tested result.

What to do when you feel like a fraud at work

Expect the wobble. Being new is uncomfortable for most people. A rough Tuesday in your first month tells you about that Tuesday, not about you.

Tell one person. The students didn’t just read that the worry was common. They put it in their own words for someone else. If you tell one colleague “I’m still mostly guessing”, you give them the chance to say “same here”.

Keep a record of what you did. For many people, the sense of being up to the job arrives after they’ve done it, not before. A written list lets you see what you’ve done well before the feeling catches up.

Be honest when you really are out of your depth. Sometimes you’ve taken on a role that stretches past what you know, and you’re learning with people watching. That’s uncomfortable, and it isn’t fooling anyone. Ask, note the answer, and pick it up again tomorrow.

Two minutes: a time you managed before you felt ready

Try this now, two minutes. Paper, or a note on your phone.

  1. Remember one occasion when you did something you didn’t feel equipped for. Your first day on a new team, a speech at a wedding, driving on the motorway for the first time.
  2. Write a line saying what you did.
  3. Add a line on how it actually went. It doesn’t need to have been perfect.

Example: Chaired my first team meeting in week two. Forgot one item on the agenda, picked it up at the end, nobody minded.

You managed it before you felt ready, and the sense of being ready came later, if it came at all.

Questions people ask

Is imposter syndrome a mental illness? No. It isn’t a medical diagnosis. It’s a popular name for a common feeling: that your success came from luck and someone will find you out.

Is imposter syndrome only something women get? The 1978 paper that gave it its name was about high-achieving women. The 2020 review found impostor feelings in both men and women.

Does it go away once you’re experienced? Not always. Someone who has done the same job for years can still have the thought after a bad day. If you’re holding off on speaking up or applying until you feel ready, the wait could be a long one.

What’s the real figure, then? There isn’t one. Across 62 studies it ran from 9 to 82 per cent, depending on how each study asked.
