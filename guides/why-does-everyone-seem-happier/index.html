Why Does Everyone Seem Happier Than Me?

Short answer: everyone seems happier than you because you’re comparing your inside with everyone else’s outside. You know your own bad days in full, and mostly you see other people’s good ones. In four studies published in 2011, people underestimated how often others feel bad. So the picture you’re measuring yourself against is likely to be brighter than the real thing.

It’s 1 a.m. and you should be asleep. Instead you’re scrolling. A new house, keys held up to the camera. An engagement. Somebody’s promotion, somebody’s holiday, somebody’s toddler being adorable on a beach.

You put the phone face down and look at the ceiling. Everyone else seems to have been handed the instructions. Everyone else seems happier.

What is wrong with me?

Probably nothing. This page is about why the comparison feels so one-sided, what the research says about it, and one small way to make it fairer.

Why does everyone seem happier than me online?

What goes online is the graduation photo and the new car. What stays offline is the overdraft that paid for the car and the sulk in the taxi after the graduation.

There’s research on what that does to the person scrolling. Hui-Tzu Grace Chou and Nicholas Edge surveyed 425 university students for a 2012 paper in Cyberpsychology, Behavior, and Social Networking. Students who had been on Facebook for more years, and who spent more hours a week on it, agreed more strongly that other people were happier than them and had better lives. The same went for students who had plenty of people on their friend lists whom they’d never met face to face.

My own reading of that last part is simple. You know your friends’ bad days, at least some of them. With people you barely know, all you have is the highlights.

It was a survey, so it shows a link, not a cause.

Why do we compare ourselves at all?

If nothing objective tells us how we’re getting on, we check ourselves against the people around us. Leon Festinger put this forward in 1954, in “A Theory of Social Comparison Processes”, published in Human Relations.

Am I doing all right for my age? Am I ahead of my cousin, behind my old classmate? Nobody hands out a scale for that, so we look sideways. These days, often at a screen.

So comparing isn’t a flaw in you. You can’t simply give it up. Seeing other people means measuring yourself against them.

Does everyone feel behind in life?

I can’t tell you everyone does. But the research suggests more people have bad days than you’d guess.

Alexander Jordan, then at Stanford, led a team that published four studies in 2011 in Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, under a title that says it all: “Misery Has More Company Than People Think.”

People guessed that others felt bad less often than they really did. They also thought of their own low moods as more hidden than their happy ones. Those who underestimated other people’s low moods tended to be lonelier and to dwell on things more, and people who overrated how good a time others were having tended to be less content with their own lives.

So think about the person sitting opposite you on the train or across the desk. They see the composed face you put on before you walked in, and some of them assume you’ve got everything sorted. Some of them are making the very same comparison you are, with just as much missing from it.

You’re not alone in this. Your own inside is simply the only one on view to you.

What does the comparison cost you?

Erin Vogel, Jason Rose, Lindsay Roberts and Katheryn Eckles, at the University of Toledo, reported two studies in 2014 in Psychology of Popular Media Culture.

The first was a survey. People who used a social media site most often tended to have lower self-esteem, and the researchers tied that to more comparisons with people who appeared to be doing better than them. In the second, students glanced at invented profiles. Some profiles showed someone thriving, busy socially and living healthily. Others showed someone faring less well. Students who briefly saw the thriving profile rated their self-esteem lower, right then, than students who saw the other sort.

That was a short experiment with made-up profiles, so treat it as one piece of evidence. In that experiment, a brief look was enough to lower how students rated themselves for that moment. Whether everyday scrolling works the same way wasn’t tested.

If those comparisons tend to come with a harsh voice attached, the page on why you’re so hard on yourself is about answering it.

How to make the comparison fair

You’ll keep comparing. The aim is to compare fairly.

Whenever a post leaves you feeling you’ve fallen behind, add back what it didn’t show. The mortgage stretched to its limit. The thirty takes before the one that went up. The tears in the hotel room on the second night. You don’t need to know the details. You don’t need to picture their life as secretly miserable either. Just remember you’re seeing the outside.

And when you can, see people face to face, in their own kitchens, where the washing up waits and the cracks are visible, the same as at yours. That’s my suggestion, not something the studies tested, but it’s the one place you get to see a bit more of someone’s inside.

Two minutes: one post, and what it leaves out

Try this now, two minutes. Start a timer.

  1. Open whichever app you use most. Scroll until a post gives you that sinking feeling: a wedding, a pay rise, a new kitchen, a trip abroad. Screenshot it.
  2. In a phone note or on paper, put today’s date and the words “What’s missing from this picture:” Then fill it in. Be generous in your guess.
  3. Tell yourself, under your breath: “I’m putting my inside next to their outside.”

Hang on to the screenshot for seven days. Then delete it, and notice whether you can still recall what stung.

Questions people ask

Why does everyone else seem to have their life together? Mostly because you only see the version they choose to show, the same way they only see yours. In the Jordan studies, people saw their own bad feelings as more private than their good ones, and underestimated how often others feel bad.

Is social media making me unhappy? The research here can’t say that for certain. The Chou and Edge study was a survey, so it shows a link, not a cause. The Vogel experiment found a brief look at someone doing better lowered how students rated themselves in that moment. Both are worth knowing. Neither is the whole story.

How do I stop comparing myself to others? You probably won’t stop altogether, and you don’t need to. Aim to make the comparison fair instead: each time you feel behind, put back the part the post left out.
