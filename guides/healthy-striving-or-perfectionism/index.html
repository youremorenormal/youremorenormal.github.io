Healthy Striving vs Perfectionism: How to Tell the Difference

Short answer: healthy striving vs perfectionism comes down to what’s driving you. Aiming high because the work matters to you is one thing. Worrying about mistakes and how you’ll be judged is another. In a 2016 review of 43 studies, the more people had of that worry, the more burnout they tended to report. Aiming high showed no such link.

It’s Sunday evening and you’re still on the report you meant to finish on Friday. The file is called report_FINAL_reallyfinal. A paragraph you were happy with this morning now reads badly, and once you’ve reworded it, the paragraph above stops sounding right. Every time the end is in sight, there’s more to do.

Is that just having high standards? Or is it something else?

Is perfectionism just having high standards?

Not quite. Researchers split perfectionism into two parts. The first is perfectionistic strivings, which in plain words means aiming high. The second is perfectionistic concerns: worry about mistakes and judgement.

Most of us have some of both. The useful question is which one is in charge of a particular task.

Healthy striving vs perfectionism: what the research found

Hill and Curran published a meta-analysis in 2016 in Personality and Social Psychology Review. A meta-analysis combines the findings of lots of earlier studies, and this one brought together 43 of them, with 9,838 people across work, sport and education, to see how each part of perfectionism related to burnout.

The more people worried about mistakes and judgement, the higher their burnout scores tended to be. Aiming high showed no such link, or a slightly lower burnout score.

That’s a link, not proof that one causes the other. The split is still useful. Wanting your presentation to land well is one thing. Redoing the slides at 2 a.m. because a colleague might raise an eyebrow is another, and it’s the second kind that goes with burnout.

Why can’t I finish anything?

There’s a pattern that might explain the report.

Shafran, Cooper and Fairburn set out an idea they called clinical perfectionism in 2002, in Behaviour Research and Therapy. It was an account rather than an experiment, and it was about people for whom this has real costs. On their account, falling short of the standard brings self-criticism, while reaching it leads the person to decide the standard was set too low. So a miss means blame, and a hit means a higher bar.

You might know a milder version. A weak draft means you carry on; a strong draft means it should have been even better. That’s how perfectionism and procrastination can end up looking like the same problem: a job with a moving finish line never quite gets sent.

Samuel Johnson knew this feeling. In 1747 he said he’d produce a dictionary of the English language, and when a friend wondered how he could manage it in three years, he told him he was sure he could. It was published in 1755, around eight years on. In his preface he compares the pursuit of perfection to chasing the sun, which always seems to sit on the next hill and is never any closer when you get there. So he limited the job, so that it “would in time be ended, though not completed”. After that he let it go, and admitted openly that people would find faults in it.

Is perfectionism increasing?

Curran and Hill followed up in 2019 with a study in Psychological Bulletin. They combined scores from 164 groups of university students, 41,641 students altogether, in the US, Canada and the UK, all of whom had completed the same perfectionism questionnaire at some point from 1989 to 2016.

Over that period, scores went up on each of the three types the questionnaire covers. Expecting a lot of yourself rose, and expecting a lot of others rose too. The largest increase was in feeling that other people expect a lot of you.

Each year’s students were different people scoring themselves, so it tells you about students rather than everyone.

How to tell which one is driving you

Before you start a task, ask yourself one question: am I doing this well because it matters to me, or because I’m afraid of someone spotting a flaw?

If the honest answer is the fear, that’s the task that needs a stopping point.

A few things that help:

  • Fix the finish line before you start. Settle what finished means before you begin, and put it in writing. A finish line that lives only in your head is easier to shift. This is my suggestion drawn from the account above, not something the studies tested.
  • Keep your best effort for the handful of things where the standard is truly your own. Most of a normal week doesn’t qualify. The council tax form and the flat-pack wardrobe need finishing, not perfecting.
  • Choose the things that deserve the extra hour on purpose. A medical form, or the wording of a eulogy. And let a thank-you text be a thank-you text.

Once you stop at good enough, there will be flaws in the work, and now and then somebody will notice one. Johnson said as much about his own dictionary: some of its faults, he wrote, would turn out to come from the very care he had taken.

Two minutes: decide where it ends, then start the clock

Try this before you begin, two minutes, then the timer. Paper, or a note on your phone.

  1. Pick one thing you keep going back to. An email, an application, an essay, a toast.
  2. Write a single line describing finished. Make it concrete. “All the boxes filled and checked once” works. “Perfect” doesn’t.
  3. Decide how much time it gets, and start a timer.
  4. When it goes off, stop. Call it finished, and send or submit it if that was the plan.

Example: “Email to the landlord. Finished means the problem, the date it started, and what I’m asking for. Ten minutes.”

If the timer goes and you feel the pull for a few more minutes, stop regardless. That pull may be the same finish line, moving again.

Questions people ask

Is perfectionism a good thing? Partly. Aiming high on its own wasn’t tied to burnout in the 2016 review. Worry about mistakes and judgement was. So it depends which part you mean.

What’s the difference between high standards and perfectionism? High standards are about the work. The part of perfectionism tied to burnout is worry about mistakes and how you’ll be judged.

Is perfectionism increasing? Among university students in the US, Canada and the UK, scores rose from 1989 to 2016 on all three types the questionnaire measures.

Why does perfectionism make me put things off? One reason can be a finish line that keeps moving. If good is never good enough, the job is never ready to send. Writing down what done means before you start gives you something firm to stop at.
